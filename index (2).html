Estimation of Minor Losses in a Pipe System

d 18 mmD 24 mmWater 28 °C

Aim

To determine the loss coefficient (K) for flow through pipes due to sudden enlargement, sudden contraction and due to pipe fittings like bends and elbow.

What you will do here. Run the bench pump, set the flow, read the water columns of a multi-tube manometer for each fitting, record h1 and h2, and work out K = 2gΔh / V². The columns settle, drift and need reading off a ruler, as on the real rig, so your numbers will differ a little each time.

After this experiment you can

  • Explain why a change in pipe area or flow direction wastes energy as eddies and heat.
  • Measure the head loss across a fitting from a pair of manometer tubes.
  • Calculate the loss coefficient K for an enlargement, a contraction, a long bend, a short bend and a 90° elbow.
  • Show that head loss varies as the square of velocity, so K stays nearly constant at high Reynolds number.

Theory

Basic concept

When fluid flows through a pipe it meets hydraulic resistances of two types: (i) viscous-frictional resistance and (ii) local resistance. Viscous-frictional resistance along the pipe is called the major loss of energy. Local resistances are called minor losses.

Local resistances come from a change of velocity in magnitude, in direction, or both. The change generates large-scale turbulence through the formation of eddies, and part of the energy of the fluid is dissipated as heat. Changes of cross-section, bends, valves and fittings of all types fall in this group. In short pipes, minor losses can be larger than the friction losses, so they must be determined.

All minor losses vary as the square of the mean velocity, so they are written as a fraction of the velocity head:

hL=KLV22g

Here hL is the loss of energy per unit weight (m), V the average velocity in the pipe (m/s), g the acceleration due to gravity and KL the dimensionless loss coefficient. KL is constant at high Reynolds number for a particular flow geometry.

Sudden enlargement

hL=(V−V1)22g

V is the velocity in the smaller pipe and V1 the velocity in the larger pipe. Using continuity this simplifies to hL = KLV²/2g with

KL=[1−A1A2]2

A1 is the cross-sectional area of the smaller pipe and A2 that of the larger pipe, both in m². For this rig A1/A2 = (18/24)² = 0.5625, so the theoretical KL is 0.191.

Why do fittings waste energy?

Pick a fitting and watch the water. Blue streaks are the main stream. Orange streaks are water trapped in eddies, where the flow energy turns into heat. The line under the pipe is the total energy of the flow, and it drops across the fitting by hL = K·V²/2g. Shapes are exaggerated and not to scale.

    Procedure

    Experimental setup

    The apparatus consists of two pipes connected through a common manifold. The pipes are fitted with a bend, an elbow, a globe valve, a gate valve and so on. A sudden enlargement and a sudden contraction are also provided in the pipe. Water is supplied to the manifold through an inlet valve in the supply pipeline connected to a constant overhead water tank. Pressure tappings on the upstream and downstream ends of each fitting give the pressure head difference across the fitting, from which the head loss is computed. The tappings are connected to a multi-tube U-tube differential manometer. The discharge through each pipe is regulated by a valve near the outlet end of each pipe.

    Procedure

    1. The flow and bench control valve is opened parallelly for maximum discharge.
    2. Note down the manometer reading and flow rate of water.
    3. The above procedure is repeated for different discharges by controlling the gate valve, and readings are noted down in the table.

    In the virtual lab

    1. Open the Simulator. Click the pump switch on the bench to start the pump.
    2. Open the red bench valve and the red valve on the rig. Click a wheel to open it by a quarter turn, drag it round, or use the sliders. The quick buttons set 10, 15 or 20 L/min for you.
    3. Wait until the readout says Steady. The digital meter on the bench shows the flow in L/min.
    4. Click a numbered fitting on the pipe, or pick it in the reader. Read h1 (upstream tube) and h2 (downstream tube) off the magnified ruler, type them in and press Record.
    5. Repeat for every fitting, then for two more flow rates. The table fills in the head loss, flow rate, velocity, V²/2g and K.
    6. If a column runs dry at high flow, use the hand pump beside the manometer board to raise the static level, then read again.

    Precautions

    • Read the bottom of the meniscus with your eye level with it. Do not read while the columns are still moving.
    • Keep the water free of air bubbles. Air in a tapping line gives a false head difference.
    • Take every pair of readings at one steady flow rate.
    Click the pump switch, then turn the red wheels (click, drag, shift+click to close, or scroll). Click a numbered badge to choose a fitting. Hover a tube for its level.
